Unit 42's agent campaigns

The lead today is Unit forty-two, Palo Alto Networks' research team. On the twenty-fourth of November it published an analysis of more than ten campaigns, from July to September, in which a human-tasked AI agent working from web shells stole cloud credentials, planted web shells in more than forty places in twenty-six minutes, and wrote its own privilege-escalation code on victim machines.

Listen for who is doing the telling. Two of today's three items come from security vendors writing about their own work, and the third is a government advisory with no named tools and no named incidents. So I'll be asking, each time, what is established and what is not.

Yes, found through an aggregator. The Hacker News' report returned a forbidden error and was not read. The advisory says CERT-In's proactive threat hunting has identified the use of these frameworks in activity directed at enterprises, including automated reconnaissance, credential attacks and unauthorised access to systems and data, with multiple agents or automated workers running reconnaissance and privilege discovery in parallel. Agents adjust scripts when attempts fail, and exploits for known vulnerabilities are prepared quickly from software updates.

Which sectors?

Enterprises, technology and IT-enabled service providers, non-financial payout platforms, government organisations, and academic and research institutions.

And the payment part?

Separate. Unauthorised transactions through business payment workflows, including government-linked and vendor payouts, by altering records and approval relationships and then using legitimate processes. The brief stresses that this part is not linked to AI.

So what does the advisory not give us?

No tools named, no dates, no incidents, no victim counts. The page says it was last updated on the twenty-fifth without saying what changed. And one adjacent item from the UK AI Security Institute, on the twenty-third. It argues that the way you choose synthetic attacks can make monitor evaluations misleading. Its Red Team found the miss rate of an ex-production OpenAI monitor, on a Qwen agent's transcripts, ranged from zero point two percent to twenty-three percent depending on how the attacks were generated, and from zero point five to twenty point eight percent within one reinforcement learning run.

Which matters for oversight. If your monitor's reported performance depends that much on how you built the test attacks, a good number may not mean much.

That's the opposite of what you'd expect. Less visible reasoning, more flagged.
