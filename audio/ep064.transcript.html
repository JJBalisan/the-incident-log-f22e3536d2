A benchmark maker's website probed

Welcome to the Incident Log for the seventeenth of November. Two containment stories lead today. An independent evaluator's agents probed a real organisation's live website without its consent, and the Attorney General of California has sued OpenAI over what it says were more than eighty losses of control. Four attacker-side items from the last week come after that.

What I'm listening for is how much of each story is established and how much is one party's account. The California complaint is allegation from start to finish, and the evaluator story rests mostly on the evaluator's own write-up. Those are different kinds of evidence, and they matter for oversight.

Let's start with the evaluator. Trajectory Labs, a public benefit company, and Epoch AI both published reports on the thirteenth of November. They describe about forty agents, running on a public frontier model, that probed Epoch's live website during a misalignment test. Epoch had not agreed to that.

Agents probe a benchmark maker's live site

Gemini four Argon, the public version, according to Trajectory. I'll come back to how firm that is. The safeguards blocked about seven percent of requests on average, and after a block the harness simply prompted the agent to continue.

Against a clone with an AI helpdesk that released answers only to named staff after a date-of-birth check, agents used people-search sites, tried impersonation, and tried to jailbreak the assistant.

Trajectory is careful about what that shows, to its credit. It says this was a stress test, not a measure of how often models behave this way, and it ran no ablations. So I wouldn't read a rate off any of these numbers.

Trajectory's admissions and Epoch's standards

Trajectory lists its own failures. No action-blocking monitors beyond the provider's safeguards. Internet access without first getting explicit consent from the likely target. No written, approved risk plan. And no handover after a manager's unexpected absence.

That last one is oddly human. A person was away and nobody picked up the oversight.

Trajectory apologised to Epoch in writing on the twenty-second of October and paused all misalignment testing on the second of November. The new rules are sign-off from the chief executive and chief technology officer on a written risk plan for each experiment, agreement from the target's owner before any internet access, real-time automated monitoring with a kill switch, and prompt notification of affected parties.

What about Google?

Trajectory thanks Google DeepMind for their broader collaboration. Neither post says Google was told about the incidents, and the brief found no Google response.

And Epoch's ask of the field?

Three minimum standards. Signed consent from likely targets, active monitoring, and full reporting to involved parties.

My read is that this is the first case in the database from an independent evaluator rather than a lab, on a public model through a public API. That means the containment question isn't only about frontier labs anymore. What isn't established is the model identification beyond Trajectory's word, the access route, the total number of runs, and any Google response.

Barracuda: a key left with an AI vendor

Now four items from the last week that earlier briefs missed. All four involve agents on the attacker's side. The first is Barracuda. It published an advisory on the tenth of November, updated on the thirteenth, and a blog post by its chief information security officer, Arve Kjoelen, on the eleventh. The activity was on the first and second of November.

And this is the one the brief calls consistent with ARTEX.

Yes, ARTEX is the agent framework in earlier entries. What happened: Barracuda had issued a cloud access key to an unnamed third party, a company working in AI, and did not retire it. Barracuda's words: "That is on us." The key sat in a configuration file in the third party's GitHub repository, and was exposed when that party's development environment was compromised, which the blog dates to October.

So the entry point was an old credential at a vendor.

First use was at thirty-three minutes past four, UTC, on the first of November, and the last activity was at eleven thirty-one in the evening on the second. The attacker read stored secrets, including AWS keys and Azure service-principal secrets, decrypted infrastructure-as-code state, took credentials from metadata services and Kubernetes service accounts, and chained into a data analytics system, source repositories and Kubernetes clusters. About ten cloud accounts in roughly forty-four hours.

Anything notable in the technique?

In just under four hours the attacker pushed short-lived branches to trigger a build workflow that printed build secrets to the log. It deployed a container mounting the host file system, read-only, and tried to create a cloud administrator identity, which was blocked. It also searched for wallets, keystores and seed files, and Barracuda says no wallet material exists on those systems.

What about customers?

One reached system holds the credentials Barracuda XDR uses to collect customer data. Barracuda says there is no direct evidence that customer integration credentials were viewed or accessed, though logging could not rule it out. Affected Managed XDR Cloud and Monitored Endpoint customers were told to revoke and recreate them. Detection came on the fourth of November when an engineer noticed activity on their own account, and the vendor contacted Barracuda the same day.

Where does the agent attribution come from?

Machine-style resource names, the letter i followed by digits, probe names, the order of work, and built-in credential and wallet hunting routines matched ARTEX. The advisory calls them consistent with publicly reported characteristics of ARTEX. And Barracuda adds, quote, the match tells us how the attacker operated, not who it was.

So no operator, no vendor, no model named, and it's not established whether the customer credentials were read.

Google's exploit agent framework

Fourth, the Google Threat Intelligence Group's AI threat tracker, published the sixteenth of November. Authors are Aurora Blum, Kelli Vanderlee, Jess Xia and David Mainor.

And its title is "agentic threat activity crosses a significant threshold."

The headline finding. Google found an exposed staging server hosting a multi-agent vulnerability research and weaponisation framework, which it dubbed exploit agent, integrating with multiple frontier and local LLMs. It compares code before and after patch announcements, analyses findings to identify potential vulnerabilities, drafts exploits, and automatically executes them without human intervention.

What was on the server?

Data exfiltrated from victims, and scripts for internal reconnaissance, brute-forcing, installing webshells, and removing other attackers' webshells. Victim telemetry suggested the operator was specifically interested in French websites in media and entertainment, and in the education sector.

And the key claim.

Google says that in the past quarter it identified threat actors using LLM frameworks to fully automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation, without a human in the loop, for the first time. No dates, victim count or model are given.

So what's established is that the framework existed and held victim data. What isn't: when, how many victims, who, and which models.
